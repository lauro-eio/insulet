## How to Use This Guide

This guide consolidates, in one place, the labor-compliance responsibilities that fall on managers overseeing employees in Mexico. It brings together previously scattered policies, FAQs, and job aids into a single, easy-to-navigate reference and serves as the primary resource for day-to-day people decisions involving Mexico-based employees.

This guide is an internal manager reference and does not replace Mexican labor law, individual employment agreements, company policies and/or arrangements, or case-specific Legal/HR advice.

### Who this is for

- Managers based outside Mexico who have one or more direct or indirect reports located in Mexico.
- HR Business Partners, People Operations, Payroll, and Legal partners who support those managers.
- New people-leaders inheriting a Mexico-based team for the first time.

### The golden rule

**When in doubt, do not act alone.**

Mexico is one of the most employee-protective jurisdictions in the world. Nearly all major decisions involving people (such as contracts, overtime, discipline, and particularly termination) should be made in collaboration with your local HR Business Partner and, if indicated, Legal. This guide explains when this is necessary.

### How it is organized

Each chapter pairs a plain-language explanation of the rule with the manager's specific responsibility, a process map or decision tree where a workflow exists, and call-out boxes that flag the highest-risk moments. Look for these markers throughout:

- **Manager Alert** — a high-risk action where a misstep creates legal or financial exposure.
- **Escalate Now** — stop and involve HR or Legal before proceeding.

This guide is an internal manager reference and does not replace Mexican labor law, individual employment agreements, company policies and/or arrangements, or case-specific Legal/HR advice.

## Chapter 1 — Why Mexico Is Different

Managers who have only led teams in the United States or in other "at-will" markets often carry assumptions that do not fully apply in Mexico. This chapter revises those assumptions prior to any decision regarding personnel being made.

### 1.1 An employee-protective framework

Mexican labor relationships are governed by the Ley Federal del Trabajo (Federal Labor Law, or “FLL”). The FLL is built around the protection of the worker. Rights are statutory, generous relative to many countries, and difficult to waive, even by mutual agreement.

Practically, this means the burden of proof in almost any dispute sits with the employer. Documentation is your protection.

### 1.2 There is no “at-will” employment

In the United States, an employer can generally end employment for any lawful reason. That concept does not exist in Mexico. While an employer may decide to end an employment relationship without a legally recognized cause, doing so generally results in an unjustified dismissal and requires payment of the applicable statutory severance.

> **MANAGER ALERT!**  
> Never say or write “at-will,” “probationary at-will,” or “we can let you go anytime” to or about a Mexico-based employee. These phrases are unenforceable against the employee and can be used as evidence of an unlawful dismissal intent.

### 1.3 The role of the CFCRL and labor courts

Mexico reformed its labor-justice system between 2019 and 2023. Disputes no longer start in the old Conciliation and Arbitration Boards. Instead:

- The **CFCRL** (Federal Center for Conciliation and Labor Registration) runs a mandatory, out-of-court conciliation stage that most disputes must pass through before any lawsuit can be filed.
- **Labor Courts** (*Tribunales Laborales*), part of the judicial branch, hear cases only if conciliation fails.

A large majority of disputes are now settled at the conciliation stage rather than in court. This makes early, well-documented, HR-led handling of any issue far more important than litigation strategy.

### 1.4 What this means for you as a manager

| Your assumption (other markets) | The reality in Mexico |
| --- | --- |
| “I can hire as a contractor to stay flexible.” | Outsourcing/contractor use is heavily restricted. Misclassification creates back-pay and benefit liability. |
| “Probation means I can part ways freely.” | Employees accrue rights and benefits from day one, including during any trial period. |
| “Overtime is a scheduling detail.” | Overtime is tightly capped and premium-paid; mishandling it is a common source of claims. |
| “If it isn’t working out, we part ways.” | Separation requires documented cause or full statutory severance, plus a conciliation step. |
| “HR will catch any problem.” | Your documentation (or lack of it) is often the key evidence. The trail begins with you. |

Managers should engage with their HRBP before the end of any trial or initial training period to assess performance, document concerns, and determine appropriate next steps. While trial periods do not eliminate employee protections, early review helps ensure that any employment decisions are handled consistently and properly documented.

> **ESCALATE NOW**  
> These rules apply to you if you manage even a single employee in Mexico, regardless of your location or the entity issuing your paycheck.

## Chapter 2 — Employment Contracts

Mexico recognizes several types of employment relationship. Choosing the wrong one, or letting the right one lapse into the wrong one, is one of the most common and costly mistakes. Managers do not draft contracts, but managers trigger the decisions that determine which contract applies.

### 2.1 Contract types at a glance

| Contract type | When it applies | Key manager-relevant rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indefinite (full-time) | The default for any ongoing role. Most Insulet Mexico employees should be here. | Presented to the employee on Day 01 for signature. |
| Fixed-term / specified-task | Only in specific, justifiable cases (e.g., a defined project, a temporary substitution, local trainee programs). | The justification must exist in fact, not just on paper, and should only be approved by Counsel. |
| Trial / initial training period | A limited evaluation window at the start of indefinite employment, where permitted. | Employee still receives full pay, benefits, and social security throughout. |
| Outsourcing / contractor (*subcontratación*) | Generally **prohibited** for a company’s core/economic activity since the 2021 reform. Allowed only for narrowly defined specialized services that are registered with the authorities. | Do not engage “contractors” to perform ongoing Insulet work. Route any such need to HR/Legal first. |

### 2.2 The fixed-term auto-conversion rule

A fixed-term contract is only valid while its justifying reason lasts. If the stated term ends but the employee keeps working, the relationship is treated by law as having converted to an indefinite (full-time) relationship, automatically, with all the protections that implies.

> **MANAGER ALERT!**  
> Always actively manage fixed-term contracts to avoid unintentional extension. If ongoing work is expected, it should be a deliberate decision that requires HR approval before the contract expires. Informally extending a fixed-term contract can quickly turn it into a permanent arrangement and jeopardize the protections associated with its fixed term.

### 2.3 Dos and Don’ts

| ✓ Do | ✕ Don’t |
| --- | --- |
| Involve HR in any non-indefinite arrangement and keep the justification on file. | Choose fixed-term to “keep options open” when the work is actually ongoing. |
| Treat trial-period employees as full employees in every respect. | Assume a trial period lets you skip benefits, overtime, or documentation. |
| Route any contractor/outsourcing idea to HR/Legal before any commitment. | Engage individuals as “contractors” to perform recurring Insulet work. |
| Flag well before a fixed-term end date if the role should continue. | Let a fixed-term contract lapse while the employee continues working. |

### 2.4 Process map — choosing the right contract

Use this decision flow when you have an approved headcount need. HR owns execution; you own the early, honest characterization of the work.

1. **Approved role identified** — You have approved budget/headcount and a defined scope of work.
2. **Is the work ongoing / part of core operations?** — If YES → indefinite (full-time) contract. This is the default and the safe choice.
3. **Is there a genuine, time-bound or task-bound exception?** — E.g., a defined project end, a maternity-leave substitution, a permitted trainee scheme. If YES → discuss fixed-term with HR and document the justification.
4. **Is this a need to “outsource” or to use a contractor?** — STOP. Route to HR/Legal. Subcontracting the company’s core activity is generally prohibited. Do not commit.
5. **HR issues the written contract** — Signed and in place on Day 1. You receive confirmation before the employee starts.

## Chapter 3 — Working Hours & Overtime

Overtime is one of the most common sources of labor claims in Mexico, and the rules differ sharply from those in other markets. They are also subject to ongoing legislative change. Managers must treat working time as a compliance matter, not just a scheduling preference.

### 3.1 Standard shifts and limits

| Shift type | Maximum length | Statutory maximum work week | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Day shift | 8 hours | 40 hours | Standard daytime working hours. |
| Night shift | 7 hours | 40 hours (starting on 2027) | Shorter limit reflects night work. |
| Mixed shift | 7.5 hours | — | Combines day and night; night portion must stay under the legal threshold. |

The standard work week is structured around these limits.

### 3.2 Overtime caps

Overtime should be manager-approved only for extraordinary circumstances prior to the work carried out and must comply with the statutory weekly and daily limits.

Current Labor Law rules allow up to 12 extraordinary hours per week, distributed up to 4 hours per day in a maximum of 4 days; any additional extraordinary time must be validated by Legal/Payroll before implementation.

Overtime must be genuinely voluntary (not assumed or pressured), authorized in advance, documented in writing, and properly tracked and compensated in accordance with applicable law and company procedures.

### 3.3 How overtime is paid

Overtime is triggered only after an employee has exceeded the applicable working-hours limits established by law and/or their employment agreement. Managers should not assume that additional hours automatically qualify as overtime; all hours worked must be reviewed against the employee's scheduled and legally permitted working time before overtime premiums are applied.

| Overtime band | Premium | Plain-language meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First 9 overtime hours in a week — authorized extraordinary time within the statutory weekly cap | 100% additional premium (“double pay”) | Paid at twice the normal hourly rate. |
| Extraordinary time exceeding the band above | 200% additional premium (“triple pay”) | Paid at three times the normal hourly rate. |
| Sunday work (*Prima Dominical*) | Additional 25% premium | Employees whose regular schedule includes work on Sunday are entitled to an additional 25% premium on that day's salary. |
| Work on a mandatory rest/holiday day | Premium in addition to pay | Employees who work on a mandatory rest day or statutory holiday are generally entitled to their regular pay plus an additional 200% premium (triple pay in total). |

### 3.4 The most dangerous overtime mistakes

> **MANAGER ALERT!**  
> Do NOT try to replace overtime pay with a one-time “bonus,” a flat allowance, comp time, or an off-record cash payment.
>
> Replacing a discretionary payment with properly calculated overtime presents legal risks and is often contested as underpayment, potentially resulting in penalties and back-pay.
>
> It is imperative to record all overtime accurately; claims such as “they volunteered” or “it was just a busy week” are not valid defenses. Maintaining precise time records is compulsory, and a lack of such records will be interpreted unfavorably against the employer.

### 3.5 Overtime authorization workflow

Use this decision flow when extra hours are needed.

1. **Need for extra hours identified** — Manager or employee identifies that work will exceed the standard shift.
2. **Manager authorizes in advance** — Overtime is approved before it is worked. Voluntariness is confirmed, not pressured.
3. **Hours are recorded accurately** — Actual overtime hours are logged in the Workday time tracking system the same day/week.
4. **Payroll applies the correct premium** — 200% up to 9 weekly OT hours, 300% beyond. No substitutions.
5. **Manager reviews before payroll cutoff** — Confirm recorded hours match reality and approvals before the pay run closes. If you have any questions about payroll cutoff dates, contact your HRBP or the country Payroll representative.
   - **5.1 Manager reviews overtime patterns** — Periodically review overtime patterns to ensure employees are not consistently approaching legal limits and that workloads remain sustainable.

## Chapter 4 — Payroll & Statutory Benefits

While managers don't handle payroll directly, their actions—such as approving hours, expenses, leaves, and schedules—directly impact payroll results. Mexico requires a set of statutory benefits that cannot be waived. This chapter explains these benefits and, importantly, distinguishes between what managers own and what HR and Finance are responsible for.

### 4.1 Core statutory benefits

| Benefit | What it is | Manager-relevant note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aguinaldo (Christmas bonus) | A mandatory year-end bonus of 30 days’ salary, paid by December each year. | Plan workloads and approvals so December payroll is not disrupted. |
| Vacation* & vacation premium | Paid annual leave plus a vacation premium on top of regular vacation pay. | Approve leave fairly and on time; do not pressure employees to forgo accrued vacation. |
| PTU (profit sharing) | Statutory employee profit sharing, distributed within a set window each year. | No action needed; managed by HR. |
| IMSS (social security) | Mandatory enrollment and contributions for health, risk, and related coverage. | No action needed; managed by HR. |
| INFONAVIT (housing fund) | Mandatory employer contributions to the national housing fund; may include payroll deductions for employee housing credits. | No action needed; managed by HR. |
| Sunday premium | An additional premium when the regular schedule includes Sunday work. | Factor into any schedule that touches Sundays; confirm with Payroll. |

\* Vacation entitlement is generally earned upon completion of the first 12 months of service. Once granted, employees typically have up to 18 months to use their accrued vacation entitlement.

### 4.2 Who owns what

| Activity | Manager owns | HR / Payroll / Finance owns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time & overtime records | Authorizing and reviewing hours accurately and on time. | Calculating premiums and processing the pay run. |
| Statutory benefits | Approving leave; planning around mandatory dates. | Calculating, funding, and disbursing benefits. |
| New-hire setup | Timely notice of start date and role. | IMSS/INFONAVIT registration and enrollment. |
| Pay questions from employees | Listening, then routing to HR/Payroll. | Providing a clear answer. |

> **MANAGER ALERT!**  
> Never approve, suggest, or tolerate any “off-payroll,” cash-in-hand, or “we’ll true it up later” arrangement. Every peso of compensation must run through proper payroll with correct contributions. Informal arrangements expose both the company and you personally.

## Chapter 5 — Performance Management & Discipline

This chapter is the foundation for Chapter 6. In Mexico, the ability to part ways with an underperforming or misbehaving employee without paying full severance depends almost entirely on documentation that you, as a manager, create over time. The trail must exist before any termination is contemplated.

### 5.1 Why documentation is your protection

Since the burden of proof lies with the employer, maintaining a clear, current record can be the key to justifying a termination and avoiding costly legal disputes. “I know what happened” is not sufficient evidence; rather, a detailed, signed record with dates is.

> **MANAGER ALERT!**  
> Documentation alone does not automatically create legal cause for termination under Mexican labor law. Performance documentation is an important tool for business decision-making, consistency, and risk management, but poor performance by itself may not always support a with-cause separation. Depending on the circumstances, performance-related separations may require a negotiated exit strategy and close coordination with Employee Relations, HR, and Legal.

### 5.2 Progressive discipline — building the record

| Stage | What good looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Set clear expectations | Role expectations, standards, and goals are communicated in writing and acknowledged. |
| Document the gap | Specific, dated facts — what happened, when, the impact — not opinions or labels. |
| Verbal then written warnings | Employee Relations will be the primary point of contact to document each step with date, specifics, and the employee’s acknowledgment or a witnessed note of refusal to sign. |
| Improvement approach (e.g., coaching plan, PIP, or other corrective measures) | Employee Relations will be the primary point of contact to assess each case and determine the most appropriate path forward. This may include a coaching plan, a formal Performance Improvement Plan (PIP), or other corrective actions, depending on factors such as severity, supporting documentation, and prior management actions. All cases should be managed in close partnership with Employee Relations. |
| Review & decide | Document outcomes at each checkpoint. Involve your HRBP before any decision to separate. |

### 5.3 The 30-day rule on cause

> **ESCALATE NOW**  
> If a specific act of misconduct could justify a with-cause separation, the law expects prompt action. Notice to the employee is generally required within a short window (commonly cited as 30 days) of the employer becoming aware of the act. Sitting on misconduct can forfeit the right to rely on it.

### 5.4 Consistency prevents discrimination claims

Treating similar situations differently is how a performance issue becomes a discrimination claim. If two employees commit the same infraction, the response should be comparable. Inconsistency, especially along lines of gender, age, pregnancy, or other protected characteristics, is read as a pretext.

- Apply the same standards and process to everyone on the team.
- Keep records factual and role-related; never reference protected characteristics.
- If you are unsure whether your response is consistent with past practice, ask your HRBP before acting.

## Chapter 6 — Termination

This is the chapter managers most need and most often get wrong. The core principle bears repeating: there is no at-will employment in Mexico. A manager should never initiate, promise, or communicate a termination alone. Every separation, including a resignation, runs through Employee Relations and your HRBP.

### 6.1 Justified vs. unjustified

| Type | What it means | Financial consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Justified (*con causa*) | Separation for a specific cause recognized by the FLL (a closed restricted legal list), supported by documentation and proper notice. | No statutory severance if the cause and process hold up, but the evidentiary bar is high. |
| Unjustified (*sin causa*) | Any separation without a documented, recognized cause, including “not the right fit,” restructuring done incorrectly, or a failed justified case. | Full statutory severance is owed (see 6.2). |
| Mutual agreement | Both parties agree to end the relationship, typically ratified before the conciliation center. | Negotiated terms; ratification gives both sides legal certainty. |

### 6.2 Severance for unjustified dismissal

Where a dismissal is unjustified, the statutory package generally includes the following components. Treat these as the shape of the exposure, not a precise calculator; the actual figures depend on salary composition and tenure.

| Component | General measure |
| --- | --- |
| Constitutional indemnity | 3 months of the employee’s aggregate daily salary. |
| Seniority-based amount | 20 days of aggregate daily salary for each year of service. |
| Seniority premium | 12 days’ salary per year of service (capped per the statutory formula). |
| Accrued/benefits due | Proportional Aguinaldo, Vacation, Vacation Premium, Saving Funds, Interest Indexation, Bonus (when applicable), and other earned amounts. |

> **MANAGER ALERT!**  
> “Aggregate” salary includes more than base pay; it can pull in bonuses, allowances, and benefits in cash or kind. This is why real-world severance often exceeds a naive base-salary estimate.

### 6.3 The mandatory conciliation process

Before most labor lawsuits can be filed, the parties must attempt settlement through the CFCRL’s pre-trial conciliation. Negotiated exits are also commonly ratified before the conciliation center, which reviews the terms, confirms the employee’s rights are respected, and, once ratified, makes the agreement binding and hard to reopen.

> **ESCALATE NOW**  
> Because conciliation resolves the large majority of disputes, a well-prepared, HR-led, properly documented separation—ideally ratified before the conciliation center—is your strongest protection. This is never a do-it-yourself manager task.

### 6.4 Termination decision tree

1. **A separation is being considered** — STOP before having any conversation with the employee. Discuss with ER and your HRBP first.
2. **Is there a documented, FLL-recognized cause?** — Review the record with HR/Legal. No solid documentation → treat as unjustified.
3. **If cause: validate evidence, notice, and timing** — Confirm the cause is on the legal list, evidence holds, and notice is within the required window.
4. **If no cause: plan a compliant exit** — ER/Legal model full statutory severance and prepare a mutual-agreement / negotiated exit.
5. **ER and Legal will route through the CFCRL conciliation** — Ratify the agreement before the conciliation center for binding certainty.
6. **ER and Legal will settle all final payments on separation** — Severance (if owed), final salary, and all accrued benefits paid per the agreed timeline.

## Chapter 7 — Anti-Discrimination, Harassment & Workplace Safety

Mexican labor laws require employers to maintain a workplace free of discrimination, harassment, and violence, and to meet specific health and safety standards. Corporate global policies cover much of this, but Mexico imposes its own mandatory protocols and obligations that managers must actively support, not just acknowledge.

### 7.1 Mandatory protocols and standards

| Obligation | What managers should know |
| --- | --- |
| Non-discrimination & anti-violence protocol | Employers must implement a protocol to prevent gender-based discrimination, workplace violence, bullying, and sexual harassment. A diversity/non-discrimination policy is a legal requirement, not optional. |
| NOM-035 (psychosocial risk) | This official standard requires identifying and preventing psychosocial risk factors, including harassment. It involves periodic questionnaires that employees must be allowed and encouraged to complete. |
| NOM-037 (Remote Work) | Establishes employer requirements for remote work, including workplace safety, ergonomic conditions, risk prevention, employee training, and mechanisms to verify that teleworking employees have adequate and safe working conditions. Includes a 3-year reimbursement fee for purchasing an ergonomic chair. |
| Forced/child-labor protocol | Employers must maintain a protocol to prevent forced and child labor. |
| Health & safety standards | General workplace safety obligations apply. |
| Substantive-equality duties | Recent reforms strengthen employer duties to promote equal treatment and provide training to prevent workplace violence, especially against women. |

### 7.2 The manager’s duty: recognize, report, never retaliate

Managers are usually the first to hear about a problem. What you do in the first hour matters enormously.

- **Recognize.** Take any report of discrimination, harassment, or violence seriously, however it reaches you.
- **Report immediately; do not investigate independently.** Any report or concern involving discrimination, harassment, workplace violence, or retaliation must be promptly reported through the appropriate channels, including your HRBP and/or Employee Relations.
- **Protect confidentiality.** Share only with those who need to know how to act.
- **Never retaliate.** Adverse action against someone who raised a concern is itself a serious violation.

### 7.3 Why inaction is so costly

> **MANAGER ALERT!**  
> An employee who suffers discrimination, harassment, or violence can terminate their employment relationship with cause attributable to the employer and claim a full statutory severance, exactly as in an unjustified dismissal. In other words, ignoring a complaint can cost the company the same as firing the person without cause, plus reputational and regulatory exposure. Complaints can also be filed anonymously through government channels.

## Chapter 8 — Consequence Matrix for Non-Compliance

This matrix makes the stakes concrete. It is a planning aid, not a precise prediction — actual outcomes depend on facts, tenure, salary, and counsel’s assessment. Use it to understand why the responsibilities in this guide matter.

| Action / inaction | Legal risk | Financial exposure | Reputational risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Misclassifying an employee as a contractor / improper outsourcing | Reclassification, authority sanctions | High — back pay, benefits, contributions, penalties | Medium |
| Unrecorded or unpaid overtime; “bonus” substitution | Inspection, labor claim | Medium–High — back pay + penalties | Medium |
| Letting a fixed-term lapse into continued work | Auto-conversion to indefinite | Medium — lost term protections, future severance | Low |
| Terminating without documented cause | Unjustified-dismissal claim | High — full statutory severance + costs | High |
| Ignoring or mishandling a harassment/discrimination report | Employee rescission with cause; authority action | High — full-severance equivalent + penalties | Very High |
| Off-payroll or cash compensation | Tax, IMSS, INFONAVIT violations | High — assessments, penalties, personal exposure | High |
| Omitting NOM-035 / mandatory protocols | STPS inspection findings | Low–Medium — fines | Medium |
| Inconsistent discipline across the team | Discrimination claim | Medium–High — severance + damages | High |

**Note:** Fines and indemnities in Mexico are frequently indexed to the UMA or to minimum-wage multiples, both of which rise annually.

### 9.2 Red flags — stop and escalate immediately

- A fixed-term contract is near or past its end date, and the person is still working.
- Overtime is happening without authorization or accurate records.
- Someone proposes a “bonus,” allowance, or cash to replace overtime or wages.
