Chapter 1 - Why Mexico Is Different
Managers who have only led teams in the United States or in other "at-will" markets often
carry assumptions that do not fully apply in Mexico. This chapter revises those
assumptions prior to any decision regarding personnel being made.

1.1 An employee-protective framework
Mexican labor relationships are governed by the Ley Federal del Trabajo (Federal Labor
Law, or “FLL”). The FLL is built around the protection of the worker. Rights are statutory,
generous relative to many countries, and difficult to waive, even by mutual agreement.

Practically, this means the burden of proof in almost any dispute sits with the employer.
Documentation is your protection.

1.2 There is no “at-will” employment
In the United States, an employer can generally end employment for any lawful reason.
That concept does not exist in Mexico. While an employer may decide to end an
employment relationship without a legally recognized cause, doing so generally results in
an unjustified dismissal and requires payment of the applicable statutory severance.



           MANAGER ALERT!
Never say or write “at-will,” “probationary at-will,” or “we can let you go anytime” to or
about a Mexico-based employee. These phrases are unenforceable against the employee
and can be used as evidence of an unlawful dismissal intent


1.3 The role of the CFCRL and labor courts
Mexico reformed its labor-justice system between 2019 and 2023. Disputes no longer start
in the old Conciliation and Arbitration Boards. Instead:

    The CFCRL (Federal Center for Conciliation and Labor Registration) runs a mandatory,
    out-of-court conciliation stage that most disputes must pass through before any
    lawsuit can be filed.

    Labor Courts (Tribunales Laborales), part of the judicial branch, hear cases only if
    conciliation fails.

A large majority of disputes are now settled at the conciliation stage rather than in court.
This makes early, well-documented, HR-led handling of any issue far more important than
litigation strategy.

2.2 The fixed-term auto-conversion rule
A fixed-term contract is only valid while its justifying reason lasts. If the stated term ends
but the employee keeps working, the relationship is treated by law as having converted to
an indefinite (full-time) relationship, automatically, with all the protections that implies.


             MANAGER ALERT!
 Always actively manage fixed-term contracts to avoid unintentional extension. If ongoing
 work is expected, it should be a deliberate decision that requires HR approval before the
 contract expires. Informally extending a fixed-term contract can quickly turn it into a
 permanent arrangement and jeopardize the protections associated with its fixed term.

Chapter 3 — Working Hours & Overtime
Overtime is one of the most common sources of labor claims in Mexico, and the rules
differ sharply from those in other markets. They are also subject to ongoing legislative
change. Managers must treat working time as a compliance matter, not just a scheduling
preference.

3.2 Overtime caps
   Overtime should be manager-approved only for extraordinary circumstances prior to
   the work carried out and must comply with the statutory weekly and daily limits.

   Current Labor Law rules allow up to 12 extraordinary hours per week, distributed up to
   4 hours per day in a maximum of 4 days; any additional extraordinary time must be
   validated by Legal/Payroll before implementation.

   Overtime must be genuinely voluntary (not assumed or pressured), authorized in
   advance, documented in writing, and properly tracked and compensated in accordance
   with applicable law and company procedures.

3.3 How overtime is paid
Overtime is triggered only after an employee has exceeded the applicable working-hours
limits established by law and/or their employment agreement. Managers should not
assume that additional hours automatically qualify as overtime; all hours worked must be
reviewed against the employee's scheduled and legally permitted working time before
overtime premiums are applied.

  3.4 The most dangerous overtime mistakes


              MANAGER ALERT!
  Do NOT try to replace overtime pay with a one-time “bonus,” a flat allowance, comp time,
  or an off-record cash payment.

  Replacing a discretionary payment with properly calculated overtime presents legal risks
  and is often contested as underpayment, potentially resulting in penalties and back-pay.

  It is imperative to record all overtime accurately; claims such as 'they volunteered” or “it
  was just a busy week” are not valid defenses. Maintaining precise time records is
  compulsory, and a lack of such records will be interpreted unfavorably against the
  employer.

   Chapter 5 — Performance Management & Discipline
   This chapter is the foundation for Chapter 6. In Mexico, the ability to part ways with an
   underperforming or misbehaving employee without paying full severance depends almost
   entirely on documentation that you, as a manager, create over time. The trail must exist
   before any termination is contemplated.

   5.1 Why documentation is your protection
  Since the burden of proof lies with the employer, maintaining a clear, current record can
  be the key to justifying a termination and avoiding costly legal disputes. “I know what
  happened” is not sufficient evidence; rather, a detailed, signed record with dates is.



              MANAGER ALERT!
   Documentation alone does not automatically create legal cause for termination under
   Mexican labor law. Performance documentation is an important tool for business decision-
   making, consistency, and risk management, but poor performance by itself may not always
   support a with-cause separation. Depending on the circumstances, performance-related
   separations may require a negotiated exit strategy and close coordination with Employee
   Relations, HR, and Legal.

5.3 The 30-day rule on cause


           ESCALATE NOW
    If a specific act of misconduct could justify a with-cause separation, the law expects
    prompt action. Notice to the employee is generally required within a short window
    (commonly cited as 30 days) of the employer becoming aware of the act. Sitting on
    misconduct can forfeit the right to rely on it.


5.4 Consistency prevents discrimination claims
Treating similar situations differently is how a performance issue becomes a
discrimination claim. If two employees commit the same infraction, the response should
be comparable. Inconsistency, especially along lines of gender, age, pregnancy, or other
protected characteristics, is read as a pretext.

   Apply the same standards and process to everyone on the team.

   Keep records factual and role-related; never reference protected characteristics.

   If you are unsure whether your response is consistent with past practice, ask your
   HRBP before acting.

“Aggregate” salary includes more than base pay; it can pull in bonuses, allowances, and

6.3 The mandatory conciliation process
Before most labor lawsuits can be filed, the parties must attempt settlement through the
CFCRL’s pre-trial conciliation. Negotiated exits are also commonly ratified before the
conciliation center, which reviews the terms, confirms the employee’s rights are respected,
and, once ratified, makes the agreement binding and hard to reopen.



           ESCALATE NOW
    Because conciliation resolves the large majority of disputes, a well-prepared, HR-led,
    properly documented separation, ideally ratified before the conciliation center, is your
    strongest protection. This is never a do-it-yourself manager task.

7.2 The manager’s duty: recognize, report, never retaliate
Managers are usually the first to hear about a problem. What you do in the first hour
matters enormously.

   Recognize. Take any report of discrimination, harassment, or violence seriously,
   however it reaches you.

   Report immediately; do not investigate independently. Any report or concern involving
   discrimination, harassment, workplace violence, or retaliation must be promptly
   reported through the appropriate channels, including your HRBP and/or Employee
   Relations.

   Protect confidentiality. Share only with those who need to know how to act.

   Never retaliate. Adverse action against someone who raised a concern is itself a
   serious violation.

7.3 Why inaction is so costly


           MANAGER ALERT!
An employee who suffers discrimination, harassment, or violence can terminate their
employment relationship with cause attributable to the employer and claim a full statutory
severance, exactly as in an unjustified dismissal. In other words, ignoring a complaint can
cost the company the same as firing the person without cause, plus reputational and
regulatory exposure. Complaints can also be filed anonymously through government
channels.
